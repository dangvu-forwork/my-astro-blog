<h1 class="rkgk-header rkgk-true-center rkgk-text-shadow">- Inception -</h1>
<p>We have grown to reflect upon the past, and we always have. Perhaps it was what slowly defined us as "ourselves". Perhaps we seek comfort in what once was, because the present day is simply too cruel for our liking.</p>
<p>No matter how we are or how we aren't, whether we are still or we are not, the world will always continue to change. As an individual who prioritizes the freedom of expression and thought above all else, to see that everything advertised and put out today as "perfectly acceptable professional work" is just a replica of the conventional "modern" design (e.g. the simplification of logos, the modernization of web design, etc.) is quite a sorry sight to witness. </p>
<p>Yes, I feel like the world is slowly losing what it once was years ago. Our hearts have slowly begun to accept the absence of that expression-filled world, where people didn't mind expressing a "personality" in their work. To me, a lot of the web designs today (like, for example, the Roblox website) have been purged of their personal feelings, of their designers' stories, and, by extension, of a true meaning behind each intricate design choice.</p>
<p>Well, I can't say that I'm alone on this thought either. The world has about 8-9 billion people constructed with different personalities, beliefs and thoughts, after all. They could probably miss the internet of the old days, or they may be looking forward to how much the "modern" design of websites could improve over time. Personally, I believe myself to be of the former line of thought.</p>
<p>When I was still a kid, I was still on Roblox doping on tycoon games like it's my life and blood. Back then, Roblox's website design didn't look like straight garbage. Back then, I was annulled <span class="rkgk-small-txt">(is that how you spell it)</span> of all of my current responsibilities and obligations. However, I took everything I had in the past as "what was naturally granted to me", and I therefore did not pay much attention to or cherished anything about my life back then in particular.</p>
<p>Okay, I'm getting off topic. The reason I went on a rant about the past was to talk about this aesthetic, after all. It was around the 2000s, where Windows designs were based off of the optimistic, clean and graceful vision of humanity's perception of technological potential. It was still a time where companies focused more on "good looking yet expressive designs" rather than "the most bland, boring and visually oppressive modernslop designs known to mankind". Alas, my rant has gone on for too long, so all I pretty have to say to sum all that I've said from the inception up to this point is that, in my opinion, "the Frutiger aesthetic was pretty good".</p>

<h1 class="rkgk-header rkgk-true-center rkgk-text-shadow">- Description -</h1>
<p>The Frutiger Aero aesthetic (referred by Wikipedia as a "design style", but I still prefer the word "aesthetic") is an aesthetic prevalent in many user-interface designs from the mid-2000s to the early 2010s, and later on would go to influence various other forms of media. Frutiger Aero is the successor of the Y2K aesthetic, which was popular from the late 1990s to the early 2000s. It was named in 2018 by Sofi Xian of the Consumer Aesthetics Research Institute off of the Frutiger typeface designed by Adrian Frutiger and the Windows Aero design language. Although this style had no common name at the time of its influence, its reflective elements and skeuomorphic nature (word for design based off real world objects, basically) also led it to be known as Web 2.0 Gloss or "Digital Dream Utopia" <span class="rkgk-small-txt">(Wikipedia)</span></p>
<p>The most important thing to note about this aesthetic was that it was used in many kinds of media from the 2000s time period, including advertisements, video game console interface and, surprisingly enough, interior design. A note-worthy usage of the Frutiger Aero aesthetic can be found in the Windows Aero design language, originally introduced in the Windows Vista operating system. <span class="rkgk-small-txt">(Wikipedia)</span></p>

<h1 class="rkgk-header rkgk-true-center rkgk-text-shadow">- Characteristics -</h1>
<p>Frutiger Aero is rooted in both technological advancements, cultural events (Y2K) and nature itself. The style often depicts the natural as directly interconnected with digital future. Common Frutiger Aero design motifs include, but are not limited to blue skies, grass, auroras, lens flares, and bokeh (out-of-focus blur) effects. Other notable elements that may appear in a Frutiger Aero aesthetic include water, the sea, aquatic life, bubbles, etc.</p>
<p>Basically, Frutiger Aero's design choice goes by the phrase "an utopia where technological advancements, efficiency and nature co-exist together in harmony", in a period of time where the negative effects of technology were still a matter of concern for many people.</p>

<h1 class="rkgk-header rkgk-true-center rkgk-text-shadow">- Resurgence -</h1>
<p>Although Frutiger Aero is still a very well known aesthetic today, its popularity and cultural significance to many people have significantly surged again in contemporary days. The resurgence itself was greatly contributed towards by Gen Z nostalgia, in addition to their possible innate rejection of modern flat designs and a longing for a more optimistic, human-centric version of technology that wasn't just plain boring old minimalism. </p>
<p>A lot of people today still mention Frutiger Aero in passing. I remember seeing a video on Twitter of a house so flooded with rainwater that even fish began moving in to splash about there. Although I believe it must be a nightmare to anyone that has the displeasure of owning that house (or god forbid, even living in that country), it is quite funny that in the comments section, someone posted an edit of that very same video but very light blue tinted while calling it the Frutiger Aero aesthetic. I don't exactly remember where I got it from anymore, but it was still quite humorous to witness, in my opinion.</p>
<p>Aside from a few jokes here and there, there's a subreddit for the Frutiger Aero aesthetic (r/FrutigerAero, although there's a subreddit for literally almost anything so I'm not too surprised), and a few website/app designs based off of the Frutiger Aero aesthetic. There's even a Frutiger Aero Archive containing a ton of things, from Flash Media, Indie Games, Videos, ... even a dedicated community too. I will leave the link to that website <a href="https://frutigeraeroarchive.org/">here</a>.</p>

<h1 class="rkgk-header rkgk-true-center rkgk-text-shadow">- Finale -</h1>
<p>It is quite the nostalgic and optimistic style. Unlike the modern trend of designs today just being the straight-up oversimplification of everything, Frutiger Aero is a design that I feel like is trying to tell you a "story" of what they believe could have been at that point in time. It is a translucently appealing design that has definitely made me feel strongly nostalgic of a past that I've never seen, nor felt physically.</p>
<p>Some people might not like it. Some might like it. They could either like the realistic imagery, or they could not. Either way, interface aesthetics of the past like Frutiger Aero were designed to appeal to as many people as possible. Although that appeal standard has unfortunately dropped to rock bottom as time slowly progressed and went on, and many designs will probably only simplify themselves even more as time goes on, I still believe that we should, at a certain moment of our time, look back and reflect upon what actually made designs good in the first place. To me, that's a combination personality and visual appeal, and to you it could be something else.</p>
<p>Just don't forget to look back once in a while. Memories are important, after all. Even if we didn't live to experience them then.</p>
