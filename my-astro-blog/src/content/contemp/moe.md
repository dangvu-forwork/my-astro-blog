<h2>Definition</h2>
<span>The word "moe" (Japanese: 萌え, possibly converted from Japanese > Latin as moé), according to Wikipedia <span class="span-small">(i know I shouldn't quote things from Wikipedia but still it's better to give a definition)</span>, refers to a strong feeling of affection (e.g. affection, adoration, devotion, etc.) expressed and shown towards fictional characters in manga, anime, video games, or other forms of media directed at the "otaku" market. Its Japanese verb moeru (萌える), a word that means "to bud" or "to sprout", was originally used within Japanese context by otaku fans to describe an internally "sprouting" feeling of protection, affection, adoration towards a character. I think (and this is just my personal opinion) that over time, the term moeru gradually evolved into the term moe.<span class="span-small">(Wikipedia Contributors, 2019)</span>
</span><br>
<span>In the context of which this blog post is based off of, I will be using "moé" not to describe the feeling of adoration itself (although it is quite important, yes), but rather specifically, the "moe artstyle" that constructs a considerably loveable character to many. <span class="span-small">(do keep in mind that "by many" here is subjective, other people might not like it as much as this guy whos typing this blog does)</span> Originally, the word moe was meant to describe an emotional response and not an artstyle, however, artists have developed artstyles involving primarily consisting of the exaggeration of delicate features (such as the eyes) in a character to elicit a "moe" response directly in the beholder of their art. As such, I will use the term "moe" leaning towards the artstyle itself, unless specified otherwise. </span><br><br>

<h2>History and Origins</h2>
<span>The origin of the term itself, frankly, is unknown. Several popular theories have arisen describing the potential origins of the term itself (e.g. the name of popular anime heroines such as Hotaru Tomoe from Sailor Moon, or Moe Sagisawa from the 1993 anime Kyōryū Wakusei), but none of them have been proven to be factually sound and correct as of 2026. Fortunately, we do at least know of when the term first became popular: it was around the 1993-1994 period among users of Japanese bulletin board systems. Particularly <span class="span-small">(quoting wikipedia)</span>, "<i>by 1994, posts targeting female voice actors using "moe" emerged, followed by a rapid surge in posts targeting two-dimensional fictional characters in 1995.</i>"<span class="span-small">(Wikipedia Contributors, 2019)</span></span><br><br>

<span>Today, everyone has probably seen a character describeable using the term "moe" in some shape or form. This is especially true in Japan; where it is widely observable, among advertisements or products available in Japan, that many corporations and companies within the contemporary age of Japan has definitely included some element of moe to sell better <span class="span-small">(read: Moe anthropomorphism)</span>. I couldn't gather recent 2026 data regarding the market for moe media, but I can assure you that it has, in some capacity, quite literally become an unstoppable economic force in Japan.</span><br><br>

<h2>Artstyle</h2>
<span>The "moe" artstyle became a properly recognizeable and well-defined artstyle around the 2000s. The aforementioned moe artstyle of the 2000s consists of round large eyes, soft facial features, small body proportions, and high-saturation color schemes.</span><br>
<span>In one way, I guess I could describe "moe" as more of an aesthetic rather than just a simple art style. As for why it evokes the "moe" feeling itself, the artstyle was designed this way to activate a human's biological caregiving instincts (large eyes, soft facial features, body proportions directly connected with our Baby Schema (Kindchenschema)), while also helping us create an intimate, accessible emotional bond with the art itself by using soft palettes and a very intimate slice-of-life feeling.</span><br>
